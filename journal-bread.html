The bread has not changed since 2011

Four ingredients, one bowl, and the same recipe for fifteen years. People assume the point is the bread. The point is not the bread.

9 March 2026 · Ana Kowalczyk · 3 min

Every Wednesday since March 2011 I have made the same loaf, and since about 2013 I have not really made it — four children make it, in a group, and I stand next to the bowl. Flour, water, salt, a little yeast. No sourdough starter, no seeds, no fashionable flour. People are sometimes disappointed by this.

The recipe is deliberately dull because a dull recipe can be remembered. A four-year-old who has done it eight times can tell you the order without being asked, and a five-year-old can tell a three-year-old, which is the part I am actually interested in.

Nobody has ever learned to cook from a recipe they could not repeat.

What the children actually do

  • Weigh the flour. Badly, at first, and we bake it anyway so they can see what badly produces.
  • Feel the water. It should be like a bath, which is a measurement every child already owns.
  • Knead for as long as they can stand, then hand it on. This is a lesson in a thing being finished by someone else.
  • Wait. Two hours, during which they go outside and forget, and come back to find it doubled, which is genuinely astonishing every single time.

Two loaves come out. One goes on the table at afternoon snack, torn not sliced. The other goes home with two children drawn out of a jar, still warm, wrapped in a cloth they bring back the following week. In fifteen years the cloth has come back roughly ninety per cent of the time, which I consider a triumph.

The recipe, since people ask

500 g strong white flour, 330 g water, 10 g salt, 5 g dried yeast. Mix, rest ten minutes, knead a bit, prove two hours, shape, prove one hour, bake at 230°C for thirty minutes with a tray of water in the bottom of the oven. That is all of it. There is a laminated card of this by the door and you are welcome to take one.

The only thing I would say, if you make it at home with your own child, is to let them do the weighing wrong. The bread will be worse and the lesson will be better.

Ana Kowalczyk has cooked at Fernhollow since 2011.
