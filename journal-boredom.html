In defence of a boring afternoon

Forty unplanned minutes sit in the middle of our afternoon. It is the part of the timetable parents ask about most, and the part I would defend last.

28 April 2026 · Marguerite Oyelaran · 4 min

On a tour last month a father stopped in the hall, looked at the printed timetable on the wall, and asked me — politely, but genuinely — what happens between 4:20 and 5:00. The answer on the sheet is one word: nothing. I have been asked about that word more than about anything else on the page.

The honest version is that nothing is doing a lot of work. It is not supervision gone slack; there are the same three adults in the garden as at any other point in the afternoon. What is absent is an adult intention. Nobody is going to start anything, suggest anything, or set anything out during those forty minutes.

A child who has never been bored in your care has never had to decide anything.

What happens is fairly consistent, and I have watched it enough times now to be able to describe the shape of it. The first ten minutes are loud and slightly aimless. Minutes ten to twenty-five are the difficult part — there is usually a complaint, sometimes a squabble, and reliably at least one child who comes and stands next to an adult in the specific way that means fix this. We do not fix it. We say something like “I know”, which is true, and we stay where we are.

Somewhere around minute thirty a thing starts. It starts small: two children and a length of hosepipe, or somebody deciding that the low wall is a shop. Within five minutes it has other people in it. By the time we call everyone in it is usually the thing they tell you about in the car, and it is almost always the thing they want to continue tomorrow.

Why we protect it

Partly because those self-started projects are the best work of the week. But mostly for a duller reason: the alternative teaches something we do not want to teach. A day with no gaps in it teaches a child that interest arrives from outside, on a schedule, arranged by an adult. That is a difficult belief to unlearn at fourteen.

It is also, I should say, the hardest forty minutes for the staff. Doing nothing visible while a child is unhappy is harder than running an activity, and it looks worse if somebody walks in. We talk about it in supervision more than anything else we do.

What it is not

  • It is not unsupervised. Ratios are unchanged and the garden is staffed exactly as it is at 3:30.
  • It is not fixed at forty minutes. On a bleak February Tuesday it has been twenty, and nobody has ever regretted that.
  • It is not a rule against comfort. A child who needs a lap gets a lap. Boredom is not the same as being left.

If you would like to watch it happen, come at four rather than ten. It is less impressive and considerably more informative.

Marguerite Oyelaran founded Fernhollow in 2009.
