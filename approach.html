What we believe, and what it costs us

Every school says it is child-led. Here is the specific, inconvenient version of what that means at Fernhollow — including the four things we are genuinely not good at.

Roughly a nine-minute read. Written by Marguerite, revised every August.

Fernhollow started in 2009 in the front two rooms of this building, with nine children and a borrowed sandpit. The thing I wanted then is the thing I still want: a place where a four-year-old can start something difficult at half past nine and still be at it when the bell would have gone somewhere else.

Almost everything we do follows from that one sentence. The long morning block exists to protect it. The low adult voices exist to protect it. Even the decision to cook lunch here rather than have it delivered comes back to it — a kitchen means children can be sent to it, and an errand is a real reason to write something down.

Children don’t need to be entertained. They need to be trusted with something difficult, and then left alone with it long enough to finish.

The six things

1. Long blocks, few interruptions

The Grove’s morning block runs from 9:30 to 10:45 and nothing goes in it. No music teacher, no photographer, no assembly. Ninety minutes sounds indulgent until you have watched a child spend the first thirty deciding what they are actually doing. The good work is almost always in the last twenty.

2. Outside is not a reward or a punishment

We go out every day. Not “weather permitting” — every day. We keep forty sets of waterproofs and about sixty boots in the hall precisely so that nobody has to make a judgement call, because the moment it becomes a judgement call it starts getting cancelled on the days the adults are tired.

3. Real tools, taught properly

There are sharp knives in our kitchen, a tenon saw at the bench, and matches at the fire circle. Children are taught to use them, one adult to two children, and are then trusted with them. We have had four splinters, two small burns and one stitch since 2009. We record all of it and we will show you the book.

4. The adults are quiet

No whistles, no countdowns, no singing at children to make them tidy up. If you need to talk to a child you walk over to the child. If a room is loud, we look at the grown-ups in it before we look at anyone else. This is the hardest thing to hire for and the first thing we train.

5. Boredom is on the timetable

Roughly forty minutes of every day are unplanned on purpose — usually the back half of the afternoon. It is reliably the part of the day parents ask about on a tour, and reliably where the best things start. The hole (see the journal) started in an unplanned forty minutes.

6. We tell you the truth

If your child had a bad day, bit someone, or spent four hours moving gravel from one bucket to another, that is what the note will say. We would rather lose a family than manage them.

Food

Ana cooks for forty-one people every day in a kitchen you can see into from the hall. Breakfast at 8:15, lunch at 12:15, snack at 2:30 and again at 3:45 for Hollow Club. It is served at the table in dishes, not plated in the kitchen, because passing a bowl to the person next to you is most of what we mean by manners.

Wednesday is bread day and has been since 2011. Two children go home with a loaf each week, drawn by lottery, and there is a laminated card of the recipe by the door that anybody can take.

We manage allergies seriously and without drama. Currently we are a nut-free site, and we have run dairy-free, gluten-free and vegan alongside the main menu for years without anybody eating a different-looking lunch.

Outside

The garden is a third of an acre, which is a great deal more than we are required to have. There is a mud kitchen, six raised beds the children plant and largely fail to weed, four hens, a fire circle, and an oak with a rope swing that predates the school by about a century.

Forest Friday takes the Grove to Alder Wood from 9:30 to 2:00, every week, all year. In February this means hot chocolate in a flask and coming back muddy to the elbow. Nobody has ever asked to stay behind.

How we know it’s working

We do not test children. What we keep instead is a running record for each child: photographs, transcribed conversations, and short written observations, two or three a week. Twice a year we sit down with you for an hour and go through it properly.

The external measure we actually watch is what first-grade teachers tell us in the November after a child leaves. What comes back, year after year, is not “they were ahead” — it is “they keep going when it gets hard.” That is the one we are trying to earn.

Safeguarding and the boring necessities

  • Every adult is background-checked and paediatric first-aid trained, renewed every three years.
  • Marguerite is the designated safeguarding lead; Hollis is the deputy.
  • Ratios are 1:4 in the Nest, 1:6 in the Grove and 1:8 in Hollow Club — better than required in all three.
  • Accidents, incidents and near-misses all go in the same book, and the book is on the shelf by the door.
  • Our full policies — safeguarding, behaviour, complaints, inclusion — are on paper in the hall and we will email them to anybody who asks.

Four things we are not good at

Every school has these. Most of them do not print them, which we have always found strange, since you will find out in the first fortnight anyway.

  • We are slow to answer email. There is one office day a week and it is Thursday. If it is urgent, telephone — someone always picks up.
  • We do not do a lot of formal early literacy. Some children leave us reading fluently and some leave us barely reading at all, and we are calm about that in a way that does not suit every family.
  • The building is old and it shows. The hall floor is uneven, the radiators knock, and there is no lift to the first-floor office. We have a plan for the last one and no plan for the first two.
  • We are expensive, and we know it. Four part-funded places a year is not a solution to that. It is what we can currently do.

If any of this sounds like an argument you would like to have in person, that is rather the point of a visit. Come on a Tuesday.
