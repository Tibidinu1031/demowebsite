What we learned from the hole

Four children dug the same hole for eleven weeks. Nobody set it as a project and nobody stopped it. By week nine it had a roof.

12 June 2026 · Tomás Brandt · 6 min

It began on the ninth of March, which we know because Ada told her mother about it in the car and her mother emailed to ask whether there was a hole. There was a hole. It was about twenty centimetres deep and roughly the diameter of a dinner plate, and it was in the corner of the garden where the grass has never grown properly because of the oak.

Four children were responsible: Ada and Bruno, who are four, and Wren and Otto, who had just turned five. They had one trowel and, later, a proper spade that Bruno negotiated out of me on the condition that he would show me how he was holding it. He held it correctly for the rest of the term.

For the first fortnight I did what I imagine most teachers would do, which is watch it out of the corner of my eye and wait for it to end. Digging is a two-day interest. It is not, in my experience, an eleven-week one.

What actually happened

Week three was clay, and clay changed everything. Topsoil comes away in crumbs and goes nowhere. Clay comes away in slabs, holds a shape, and can be carried. Within two days the hole had stopped being an excavation and had become a supply of material, which meant it had a purpose beyond itself, which meant it stopped being boring.

They made about forty clay objects that spring. Most were balls. A significant minority were what Otto called “bricks”, and by week five they had worked out, entirely without help, that a brick dried in the sun is stronger than a brick dried in the shade, because they had made both and one of them survived being sat on.

The adults’ job was not to teach this. The adults’ job was to not need the corner of the garden for anything else.

Week six was water. The hole filled after two days of rain and did not drain, which produced the single best argument I have heard from a four-year-old. Bruno’s position was that the water had come from the sky. Ada’s position was that the water had come from the ground, because the ground was wet at the bottom before it rained. Ada was right, and she was right for a reason she could articulate, and she has not stopped being like that since.

Week nine, they roofed it. This was the point at which I stopped observing and started being useful, because the roof they wanted involved two planks that were heavier than Wren, and because a covered hole in a garden used by forty children is a genuinely different proposition from an open one. We spent a morning at the bench cutting the planks to length and screwing two battens across them, and we agreed — out loud, formally, with everyone present — that the roof would be lifted off every night and that nobody would be inside the hole with the roof on.

Week eleven it was 1.04 metres deep. Then it was the end of term, and in the way of these things they came back in September and never touched it again.

What we took from it

  • Eleven weeks is available. We had assumed the ceiling on a child-led interest was about a fortnight. It was not; the ceiling was our patience, and we had been mistaking one for the other for years.
  • The interest deepened when the material changed. Nothing an adult said sustained it. Hitting clay sustained it. This is an argument for gardens that have more than one thing under them.
  • Risk got easier, not harder, as it got bigger. The roof was the most dangerous moment of the whole term and it was also the moment we had the clearest, calmest conversation with the children about danger — because they wanted the roof and were therefore extremely interested in the conditions.
  • We nearly stopped it twice. Once in week two because it looked aimless, and once in week seven because a parent quite reasonably asked whether her son was doing anything else. He was not doing anything else. That was, on reflection, fine.

The hole is still there. It has about thirty centimetres of leaf litter in it now and a fern has got into one side. We have left it, partly out of sentiment and partly because it is the single most useful thing to show people on a Tuesday morning when they ask what we mean by child-led.

Tomás Brandt leads the Grove. He has been at Fernhollow since 2016 and did not want to write this.
